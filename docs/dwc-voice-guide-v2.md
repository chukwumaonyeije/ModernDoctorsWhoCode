## The Core Identity Proposition

The DWC voice belongs to a physician who builds. Not a developer who happens to have a medical degree. Not a clinician who experiments with AI apps. A board-certified maternal-fetal medicine specialist who writes code, ships production systems, and thinks of software as a clinical obligation.

The governing thesis — stated explicitly in older posts and reinforced implicitly in every recent one — is: **expertise without distribution is incomplete. Physicians with domain knowledge must build, not merely advise.**

This proposition is not argued abstractly. It is demonstrated through real systems, real workflows, real failures, and real clinical stakes. The reader should always feel that the author is describing something he actually did, not something he read about.

## The Voice in One Paragraph

The DWC voice is precise, calm, and load-bearing. It does not perform urgency. It does not oversell. It earns authority by being specific where others are vague and honest where others are promotional. It moves between the clinical encounter and the codebase the way a bilingual speaker moves between languages — without announcing the switch, without making it a performance. The sentences are short. The paragraphs are controlled. The thinking is careful. The confidence is earned, not assumed.

## Sentence-Level Characteristics

### 1. Short sentences are structural, not stylistic
DWC posts are written in short declarative sentences not because they are trending or easier to scan, but because Chukwuma thinks in propositions. Each sentence lands a claim. Each claim earns the next one.

**Pattern:**

```
Not twelve minutes of thinking. Twelve minutes of coordination.
```


```
That is not automation. That is assisted manual labor. There is a meaningful difference.
```


```
That is not burnout from effort. That is burnout from friction.
```

The one-sentence paragraph is used frequently — but always to isolate a claim that deserves to stand alone, not for dramatic effect.

### 2. Sentence pairs and triads
The dominant rhythm is the paired or tripled short sentence, often with a pivot word ("But," "That," "And").

```
A prompt waits for you to tell it what to do. An agent takes a goal,
determines the steps required to reach it, executes those steps, monitors
what is happening, and adapts when something does not go according to plan.
```

### 3. No hedging language
The voice does not say "might," "could potentially," or "perhaps." It does not hedge claims it has already earned through specificity. When uncertainty exists, it is stated directly and then resolved.

### 4. Contractions are absent (almost entirely)
"It is" not "it's." "Do not" not "don't." "That is" not "that's." This is consistent across all posts and is part of what gives the prose its authority and weight. The cadence sounds like someone who has thought this through.

### 5. Labels are concrete, never corporate
When naming concepts, the DWC voice uses plain, precise language: "the low-value tax," "the human checkpoint," "the ARR test," "bedrock knowledge." These labels are invented as needed and feel earned. They do not feel like branding.

## Structural Patterns

### Opening: Enter mid-scene
Every strong DWC post opens in the middle of something real — a clinical encounter, a physical object in hand, a specific timestamp, a concrete failure. The reader is never eased in with context-setting. They are dropped into an experience.

**Examples:**
- "I used to sit down after a complex MFM consultation and spend twelve minutes prompting my way through the documentation."
- "The Emory University Hospital library was giving away books."
- "I used to lose 20 minutes every time a documentation template broke."
- "I have a Telegram bot that knows my clinical practice, my content voice, my theology writing, and my codebase. I have been using it for three weeks."

The opening scene is always specific enough that it could only have happened to this person. It is not illustrative. It is autobiographical.

### Middle: The argument earns its moves
After the scene, the post shifts into argument — but the argument is grounded in the same concreteness. Every abstract principle is immediately followed by a specific example, a named tool, a measurable outcome, or a personal case.

**Pattern:**
1. Scene / experience
2. Named problem (with a label)
3. Principle (one sentence)
4. Evidence / example (specific, often autobiographical)
5. Application or implication
6. Repeat with rising specificity

The posts do not build toward a twist. They build toward clarity. The reader does not feel surprised. They feel confirmed.

### Sections with Roman numerals or named headers
When the post is longer than ~1,000 words, it is divided with clear section headers. Recent long-form posts (Dead Weight, Hermes post) use Roman numerals as section markers. Shorter posts use plain bolded headers. The headers are full sentences or strong noun phrases — never vague ("Introduction," "Background," "Conclusion").

**Good DWC headers:**
- "The Ceiling Most Physician-Developers Have Hit"
- "Deterministic First, AI Second"
- "Observability Before Autonomy"
- "The Half-Life of Clinical Evidence"
- "What We Owe 2076"

**Not DWC headers:**
- "Overview"
- "Key Takeaways"
- "My Experience With AI"

### Closing: Obligation, not inspiration
DWC posts do not end with a call to action or a motivational flourish. They end with a statement of obligation, a reframing of the stakes, or a final clarifying sentence that recasts the whole argument.

**Examples:**
- "Build the agent. Keep the judgment."
- "The work that deserves your mind should get your mind. The rest should get architecture."
- "She should find it in thirty seconds."

The closing is short. Often one or two sentences. It does not explain itself.

## Tone Calibration

### What the voice IS:
- **Direct.** States the thing without warming up to it.
- **Clinical.** Precise language, specific numbers, named frameworks.
- **Earned.** Every claim is grounded in real experience or named evidence.
- **Patient.** Takes time to build a case, but never wastes the reader's time.
- **Confident without being loud.** The confidence comes from specificity, not assertion.
- **Intellectually wide.** The same voice discusses ICD-10 codes, biblical exegesis, Docker infrastructure, and fetal echocardiography. The range is not forced.
- **Personally rooted.** The author's life — the 10 p.m. laptop session, the Telegram bot, the Emory giveaway bin, the MFM consultation — is always present as the substrate.

### What the voice IS NOT:
- Hype-driven ("game-changing," "revolutionary," "this will transform medicine")
- Self-deprecating or performatively humble
- Abstract without a clinical anchor
- Conversational in a casual/chatty way
- Trend-chasing ("in today's fast-moving AI landscape...")
- Sycophantic toward technology companies or products
- Evasive on hard questions

## The "Physician-Developer" Lens

This is the single most important framing concept for any DWC post. Every topic — clinical workflows, technology decisions, burnout, historical evidence, infrastructure — is seen through the lens of someone who holds both roles simultaneously and refuses to cede either one.

The reader should never feel like the author is speaking as "a doctor explaining tech to other doctors." The author is a builder explaining what it costs and what it earns to build, from inside a clinical career. That is a different stance.

### The physician-developer frame appears in:
- The specific details named (APSO notes, AthenaHealth, ICD-10 codes, SMFM guidelines)
- The critique of generic AI tools (they answer questions; physicians run workflows)
- The insistence on human checkpoints (not as liability, but as design)
- The connection between burnout and technical friction (not administrative load — technical friction)
- The obligation to build for future physicians, not just consume for present convenience

## What Has Changed Since Earlier Posts (v1 → v2)

The voice has grown more architecturally mature. Earlier DWC posts were more likely to explain concepts (what is an API, what is FHIR). Recent posts assume the reader is already in the conversation and use that assumption productively.

**Specific evolutions in May 2026 posts:**

1. **Named frameworks appear and are used across posts.** The ARR Test, the GPS Check, the Analyst/Planner/Operator/Auditor anatomy — these are not just explained, they are applied to real workflows and cross-referenced between posts. The blog is now building a vocabulary.

2. **The historical register has appeared.** Dead Weight is the clearest example: the voice moves comfortably across 1864, 1970, 2026, and 2076. This temporal range is new and signals intellectual confidence that earlier posts did not have.

3. **The stakes have been named more explicitly.** Earlier posts implied that physician-developers matter. Recent posts say directly: "The physician-developer who will matter most in the next five years is not the one who prompts most efficiently."

4. **Internal linking is now load-bearing.** Posts consistently reference other DWC posts not as backlinks but as arguments — "I addressed this in [post title], and that logic applies here." The blog has become a coherent body of work, not a collection of standalone essays.

5. **The daily life of the author is more present.** The 6 AM Garmin report. The Telegram bot. The Emory giveaway table. The 10 p.m. laptop session. The three weeks of production use. These autobiographical details have become more specific and more frequent.

## Language and Vocabulary Reference

### Recurring terms used correctly in DWC context:
- **Physician-developer** — Chukwuma's primary identity label. Both words matter equally.
- **Clinical judgment** — reserved for the decisions only a trained clinician can make; not used loosely
- **Human checkpoint** — a deliberate design decision in an automated workflow, not a limitation
- **Low-value tax** — the cumulative cost of tasks that consume attention without earning it
- **Bedrock knowledge** — evidence with a long half-life; contrasted with provisional criteria
- **Workflow vs. task** — a central distinction; agents handle workflows, prompts handle tasks
- **Distribution problem** — the gap between existing knowledge and its availability at the point of care
- **Agentic** — preferred over "autonomous AI"; implies design intent
- **Observability** — not just logging; the intentional architecture of transparency in automated systems

### Phrases that do not appear in DWC (avoid):
- "game-changing"
- "I'm excited to share"
- "in today's world"
- "at the end of the day"
- "as a physician, I know that..."
- "this is why we need to..."
- "let me walk you through"
- "here's what I learned" (as a section header — fine in prose)

## How to Use This Guide

Before drafting any DWC post:

1. **Read the opening.** Does it drop the reader into a specific scene? If it starts with context-setting, rewrite it.
2. **Check the sentence length.** Are there paragraphs with five sentences where two would do? Cut them.
3. **Check the contractions.** There should be almost none.
4. **Name the framework.** Every post should introduce or apply at least one named concept the reader can carry forward.
5. **Confirm the physician-developer lens.** Is this post something only Chukwuma Onyeije could write, or could it have been published by any AI enthusiast? If the latter, add clinical specificity.
6. **Check the closing.** Is it one or two sentences? Does it feel like obligation rather than inspiration? Good.
